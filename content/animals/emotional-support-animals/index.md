## What Is an ESA?

- An Emotional Support Animal or ESA is a companion animal that provides therapeutic benefits to an individual with a mental health condition
- They do not perform specific tasks but offer comfort through their presence

## How to Get an ESA

1. Consult a mental health professional
   - Your therapist or doctor can assess your need for one
2. Obtain a letter
   - You will need a letter from your therapist or doctor stating you have a disability and that your animal provides therapeutic support

## 3 Key Requirements to Become an ESA

1. They are domesticated
   - Must be considered a species that is domesticated and kept as a pet
2. Manageable
   - Must be able to be controlled in public settings and not be a problem around or in the home
3. Professional letter
   - A letter by a mental health professional documenting the person having a mental or emotional need for the animal

## Rules of Having an ESA

1. Understand Limitations
   - A doctor's note does not turn an ESA into a service animal
2. Rights and Regulations
   - The law says landlords must let people have approved ESAs, even if pets are not usually allowed.
3. Travel
   - Air travel policies for E.S.A.'s vary by airline and do not have the same public access rights as service animals and therapy animals.
4. Public Access
   - ESAs are not covered by the Americans with Disabilities Act which means that they do not have the same rights as service animals.
5. Employment
   - Employers may be required to provide reasonable accommodations on a case-by-case basis


## The Cost of Getting and Training an ESA

- Adoption fees for a rescue animal typically range from $50 to $200, but costs can vary depending on the organization and the animal's age or breed
- Obtaining the letter usually requires an initial consultation and assessment with a licensed mental health professional, which typically costs between $150 and $200.
- To have your pet be an ESA, the law says it just needs to behave well, not hurt anyone, and not break things. Most people teach their pets basic good manners. Group classes usually cost $250 to $500 for 6 to 8 weeks. Private lessons cost $75 to $150 each time

## Can Insurance Cover the Cost?

Private health insurance, Medicare, and Medicaid will not pay to purchase, adopt, or train an ESA. ESAs do not require specialized training by law, so training fees are strictly out-of-pocket

## Places to get an ESA

There are no places where you can buy an already trained ESA. Once you have the letter, you have to go to a shelter or rescue and pick your animal and from there train it.

## Fun Fact!

- There are no actual government databases to register an animal at to be an ESA
- Any ID cards or vests you get do not grant you and your ESA legal rights or access

## ESA Rules by Airlines

- American Airlines
  - No longer accepts ESAs in the cabin and requires them to travel as regular pets
- Southwest Airlines
  - Only lets trained service dogs to travel with passengers for free. Other animals can be transported for a small fee
- Delta Airlines
  - ESAs are not allowed on planes and must travel as regular pets, subject to pet travel fees and restrictions
- United Airlines
  - No longer recognize ESAs. They are now treated as pets and must travel under the airline's pet policy
- JetBlue Airlines
  - No longer recognizes ESAs. Instead, ESAs must travel as pets subject to their pet policy and fees
- Frontier Airlines
  - No longer accepts ESAs and requires them to travel as standard pets, subject to fees and carrier restrictions

Though most Major Airlines now treat ESAs as standard pets some international airlines like LATAM Airlines, Volaris, and Air Canada still accept ESAs in the cabin

## Can ESAs Go in Restaurants and Stores?

No, ESAs are not allowed in restaurants and stores because they do not have the same public access rights as service animals

## What to Do When You See an ESA in Public

- Never pet, talk to, or approach an ESA without first asking the handler. Even if they are not performing a "task," they are providing essential emotional comfort and should not be distracted.
- Direct your conversation to the handler rather than the animal.
- If the handler says "no" to an interaction, respect it. The animal may be helping the person manage anxiety or a phobia that makes social interaction difficult at that moment.
- Maintain a respectful distance with your own pets to avoid causing the ESA stress or distraction
